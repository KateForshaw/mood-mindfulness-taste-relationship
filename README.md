# Mood, Mindfulness & Taste Perception – Psychology BSc Dissertation

## Tools & Skills
- **Software:** IBM SPSS Statistics
- **Research:** Experimental design (repeated measures, counterbalanced), questionnaire and rating-scale data collection
- **Statistics:** Descriptive statistics, normality testing (skewness and kurtosis), paired-samples t-tests, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, Pearson's and Spearman's correlations
- **Skills:** Hypothesis testing, choosing between parametric and non-parametric methods, interpreting and reporting results

## What I Did
This was my dissertation for my undergraduate Psychology degree at Bangor University. It asked whether our mood changes how strongly we taste food, and whether everyday (trait) mindfulness is related to taste perception and how much we eat.

- **Design:** 36 university students attended two lab sessions. In one session I induced a positive mood and in the other a negative mood, using an autobiographical memory task, with the order counterbalanced. Data was collected alongside a team of fellow students.
- **Data collection:** Participants rated hunger and mood on visual analogue scales, then tasted and rated five plant-based snacks for sweet, sour, salty, bitter and spicy tastes. I also recorded how much of each snack they ate, and participants completed the 15-item Mindful Attention Awareness Scale (MAAS).
- **Analysis:** I checked normality using skewness and kurtosis, then used paired-samples t-tests (or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests where data was skewed) to compare conditions, and Pearson's (or Spearman's) correlations to test relationships with mindfulness.

## Key Findings
- Baseline hunger and mood did not differ between sessions, so the conditions were well controlled.
- The positive mood induction worked (t(35) = −6.21, p < .001) but the negative induction did not (p = .95).
- Mood had no significant effect on taste ratings or on how much participants ate, so the study did not replicate earlier findings.
- Higher mindfulness was linked to rating Skittles as sweeter in a positive mood (p = .02).
- Unexpectedly, more mindful participants ate more of the salty snacks: rice crackers in both moods (r = .37 and .41) and tortillas in a negative mood (r = .38). This challenges the idea that mindfulness reduces snacking.
